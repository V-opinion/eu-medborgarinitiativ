Religion and democracy

How do they work together or do they not work together at all?

This exploration examines the complex relationship between religion and democracy through the lens of contemporary European politics. We investigate religious Christian parties in the EU parliament and the Christian Democratic Party in the Swedish parliament who make our laws, witness personal stories of religious transition related to democracy, and confront the challenging question of the incomplete separation between state and church and between religion and politics.

Rather than offering simple answers, this project invites critical reflection on how religious and democratic values intersect, sometimes harmoniously, often tensely, in the institutions and policies that shape our societies.

Religious Christian parties in the EU parliament

...and the Christian Democratic Party in the Swedish parliament who make our laws!

Religious Christian parties play a significant role in European legislative bodies, influencing laws and policies that affect millions of people. These parties—including the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) in Germany, the Christian Democratic Centre (CDA) in the Netherlands, and the Swedish Christian Democrats—bring explicitly religious perspectives to secular governance.

The question of whether this is democratic strength or democratic tension remains contested. On one hand, these parties represent voters who wish their values to influence governance. On the other, they raise questions about the proper role of religious doctrine in secular lawmaking, particularly on issues of individual rights, reproductive freedom, and LGBTQ+ protections.

In Sweden, the Christian Democrats' influence in parliamentary coalitions has made these debates particularly visible and consequential. When religiously inspired parties hold legislative power, the incomplete separation between state and church becomes not theoretical, but immediately practical.

This section examines how religious Christian parties navigate the tension between advancing their faith's values and operating within democratic processes designed to serve all citizens, regardless of belief.

Key Actors

EU Level

Multiple Christian Democratic parties hold seats in the European Parliament, often coordinating on values-based issues. The European People's Party (EPP) serves as their main political grouping.

German CDU

One of Europe's largest Christian Democratic parties, the CDU has shaped German politics for decades with both conservative and progressive wings debating the role of Christian values.

Swedish Democrats of Christianity

The Swedish Christian Democrats have experienced growing influence, particularly in recent parliamentary coalitions, making church-state questions immediately relevant to Swedish law.

Dutch CDA

Long a coalition partner in Dutch government, the CDA represents a model of how religious parties navigate secular governance while maintaining faith commitments.

A female Christian Syrian and the question of democracy

That a female Christian Syrian whose religious community opposes introducing democracy in the same way as the church election has left her religious community to be accepted as a priest in a Protestant religious community.

This case study presents a profound intersection of religion, democracy, and individual conscience. A woman raised in a Christian Syrian community faced a choice between remaining within her tradition or seeking a faith community that aligned her religious calling with democratic values.

Her original community's resistance to democratic governance—particularly their opposition to electing leaders through democratic processes—created a fundamental conflict with her values. For her, accepting a calling to priesthood required not just theological alignment, but institutional alignment with democratic principles.

By joining a Protestant community in Northern Europe that practices democratic governance alongside faith, she resolved this tension. Yet her story raises deeper questions: Must religion and democracy be in tension? Can religious institutions flourish within democratic structures? And what does it mean when individuals must leave their cultural and religious roots to find communities that honor both faith and democratic principles?

This personal narrative illustrates how the abstract question of religion and democracy plays out in individual lives and consciences. It challenges us to consider whether the current incomplete separation between state and church also creates incomplete separations between faith and individual freedom.

This story, though illustrative of broader themes, represents one person's experience of navigating religious tradition and democratic values. It acknowledges both the strength of religious communities and the importance of democratic principles in human flourishing.

Democratizing or Converting?

What makes a female Syrian Christian in Sweden in 2026 leave the Syriac Orthodox Church and convert to a Protestant denomination?

The personal story presented earlier frames the question through the lens of conversion: a woman seeking priesthood finds acceptance in a Protestant community that welcomes her as a religious leader. Yet this narrative, while deeply personal and understandable, reflects a choice that may deserve scrutiny and reframing.

The underlying question is not simply: "Which denomination will accept me?" but rather: "Should I leave my community to find acceptance elsewhere, or should I stay and fight to transform the very structures that exclude me?"

The Central Tension:

Is conversion the answer to a lack of democracy within a religious community, or is conversion itself a failure to exercise the democratic agency that should exist within all faith communities?

The Case for Remaining and Democratizing

Nordic Protestant churches—Swedish, Norwegian, and Danish—have demonstrated that Christian communities can embrace democratic governance while maintaining deep theological traditions. Church elections, congregational participation, and democratic decision-making structures are not foreign to Christianity; they represent a particular model that many European denominations have successfully adopted.

The Syriac Orthodox Church, like many ancient Christian traditions, maintains more hierarchical governance structures. Yet this is not inevitable. Just as Nordic churches have evolved, so too could other Christian communities. A female Christian Syrian seeking to serve as a priest has two theoretical paths:

  1. 1 Conversion: Leave her denomination, accept the loss of her cultural and religious heritage within that specific tradition, and find acceptance in a more democratic Protestant community. This solves her individual problem but concedes that the Syriac Orthodox Church cannot change.
  2. 2 Democratization: Remain within her denomination and work—alongside others—to introduce democratic governance structures, church elections, and lay participation in leadership decisions. This honors her heritage while transforming it from within.

The second path is more difficult. It requires confronting tradition, building coalitions within one's community, and potentially facing resistance from religious authorities. Yet it has historical precedent: many Christian communities have successfully democratized their governance while preserving their theological integrity.

When we celebrate her conversion as a victory—a woman finally accepted as a priest—we may inadvertently reinforce the message that acceptance requires departure. What if, instead, she and others like her demanded that their own denomination evolve to meet the democratic standards that Nordic churches have established?

A Reframing:

Rather than asking "Which church will accept me?", the more challenging and potentially transformative question is: "How can I work within my own tradition to build the democratic structures that would make priesthood accessible to women and all believers equally?"

This section is not meant to shame or judge anyone's personal religious journey. Religious choice is deeply personal, and those who have found spiritual home in Protestant communities have made meaningful decisions. Rather, it is an invitation to consider whether there is also a prophetic role for those who might stay and demand that their own traditions evolve—to become not refugees from their faith communities, but agents of democratic transformation within them.

The question "Democratizing or Converting?" reflects a broader tension in contemporary religion: Do we adapt ourselves to existing institutions, or do we work to remake our institutions to reflect our values? The answer matters not just for individuals, but for whether diverse Christian traditions can adapt to democratic principles while preserving their identities.

The incomplete separation between state and church

...and between religion and politics.

Legal Structures

Many European countries maintain constitutional provisions referencing religion or God, establish state churches, or provide religious education in public schools. These legal structures reveal the incomplete separation of institutions.

Cultural Influence

Beyond law, religious values profoundly shape political culture, campaign rhetoric, and policy priorities. Politicians invoke religious frameworks even in avowedly secular democracies, revealing cultural entanglement.

Ethical Questions

Should religious voters bring faith-based reasoning to political participation? Can democracies accommodate religious perspectives without privileging particular faiths? How do we balance religious freedom with secular governance?

The incomplete separation of religion and politics is not necessarily a failure of either institution. Rather, it reflects the reality that in pluralistic democracies, citizens bring their whole selves—including religious convictions—to civic participation. The challenge lies in creating structures where this integration serves justice and inclusion for all, not just those whose religious perspectives align with governing majorities.
